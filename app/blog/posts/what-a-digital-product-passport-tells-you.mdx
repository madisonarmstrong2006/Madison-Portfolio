Pick up a coat in a store and the label tells you the size, the fabric, and how to wash it. It does not tell you where the wool came from, who made it, or what to do with it when you are done.

A digital product passport is designed to fill that gap. It is a tool that gives information about a product's origin, production, longevity, environmental impact, and luxury authenticity, viewed through a scannable tag such as a QR code or an RFID chip.

## Why shoppers want it

For my retail technology proposal, three numbers stood out.

<Stat value="40%">of Millennials and Gen Z say they are hindered from accessing the information they need about where and how their clothing is produced.</Stat>

<Stat value="54%">of US and UK consumers prefer that the fashion industry be sustainable.</Stat>

<Stat value="74%">of consumers say they are willing to pay more if their garment is fully traceable and sustainable.</Stat>

Taken together, they describe a customer who is curious, values sustainability, and is currently left to do the research on her own.

## What changes for the brand

A passport keeps brands accountable for how they source and produce their garments. It also guides customers on how to properly dispose of a piece based on the materials used, which closes a loop that most labels leave open.

## What changes for the shopper

I tested the idea against the customer archetype I built for the Reflora journey map, Mason Livermoore, a 25-year-old Manhattan merchandising director who cares about sustainability and quality. For her, a passport would:

- Show the sustainability and longevity of a garment at a glance
- Let her learn about quality and economic effects without doing research online
- Hold the brands she is considering accountable
- Let her shop in person and still know the story of every garment

That last point matters. Online, a brand can tell its story across a whole website. In a store, a tag is often all you get, and a passport turns that tag into a conversation.
